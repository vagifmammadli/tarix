# Visitor Experience and Exhibition Content

## Exhibition format

This is neither a game nor a slide deck. Visitors move through a museum route, explore maps and exhibits, and open the evidence and sources behind a claim when they want more detail.

## Visit modes

**Self-guided:** visitors move between the map, timeline, and topics in any order.

**Alongside the presentation:** the presenter shares a QR code or short link to a specific section. The first release does not need live device synchronization.

## Proposed exhibition route

1. **Opening: the earliest traces of wine** — What does “earliest evidence” mean?
2. **From grapes to wine** — Distinguish cultivation, fermentation, vessels, and production evidence.
3. **Early evidence in the South Caucasus** — Compare examples from Georgia, Iran, and Armenia by date and evidence type.
4. **Archaeological traces in Azerbaijan** — Show Göygöl, Nakhchivan, Kültepe, and other sites only when supported by sources.
5. **The medieval period** — Distinguish viticulture, wine production, and wine consumption.
6. **Göygöl/Helenendorf** — German settlement and commercial winemaking in the 19th century.
7. **The Soviet period** — Expansion and the impact of the 1985 campaign.
8. **The modern period** — Local grape varieties, revival, and the present-day industry.
9. **Closing: how to read evidence** — Examples of strong evidence, cautious interpretation, and open questions.

The route may change as research develops. Do not treat unverified dates as established content.

## Exhibit card structure

Each exhibit should include:

- Name, period, and location.
- A plain-language summary of 40–80 words.
- Why it matters.
- Find type and dating method.
- Evidence strength and limitations.
- Direct source link, author, and publication details.
- Source and license for an image or model.
- A clearly labeled static alternative when no 3D view is available.

## Interactions

- Selecting a map point opens its explanation.
- Selecting a period on the timeline reveals relevant sections.
- Visitors can rotate and zoom a 3D object and select annotated areas.
- A “What does this find prove?” card separates evidence from interpretation.
- Consider phone and large-screen presentation layouts separately.

## Live-presentation guidance

Section links should open with one tap. Keep the main explanation short; open sources and extended context separately. If visitors lose the presenter’s place, provide a “return to the current section” control.
