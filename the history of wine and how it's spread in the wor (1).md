# the history of wine and how it's spread in the world and especially Azerbaijan. Also about the history of azerbaijani wine since the first wine

Wine likely began in the wider Caucasus–northern Near East region roughly 8,000 years ago, and Azerbaijan sits at the heart of that early winemaking zone. Its own wine story stretches from Neolithic and Bronze Age evidence, through medieval restrictions under Islamic rule, to German-led commercialization in the 19th century and a modern revival since the 2000s.[^1_1][^1_2][^1_3]

## Where wine began

The earliest known traces of grape wine come from the South Caucasus and nearby northwestern Iran. Chemical residues in pottery from Georgia’s Shulaveri-Gadachrili area date to about 6000–5800 BCE, while jars from Hajji Firuz Tepe in Iran date to roughly 5400–5000 BCE.[^1_2][^1_4][^1_5]

The oldest known purpose-built winery is Areni-1 in Armenia, dated to about 4100 BCE; it included a press, fermentation vessels, storage jars, and chemical evidence of red wine. This does not mean wine was “invented” in one village—rather, grape domestication and fermentation likely developed across the Caucasus, eastern Anatolia, the Levant, and northwestern Iran.[^1_6][^1_7][^1_8]

## How wine spread

Wine spread along trade, migration, and imperial networks from its Caucasian and Near Eastern heartland.

- **Mesopotamia and Egypt:** Wine became a prestige drink in elite and religious settings; Egyptian evidence shows its use by rulers and in funerary contexts.[^1_9]
- **Greece and the Aegean:** Greeks integrated wine into daily life, symposia, trade, and religion, spreading vines across the Mediterranean.[^1_9]
- **Roman world:** Rome expanded viticulture through Europe—especially present-day France, Spain, Italy, Germany, and the Balkans—creating many of the foundations of modern European wine regions.[^1_9]
- **Islamic world:** After the spread of Islam, alcohol was restricted in many Muslim societies, but viticulture and wine knowledge persisted in some regions, often for medicinal, elite, minority-community, or local traditional uses.[^1_3]
- **Global expansion:** European colonization carried *Vitis vinifera* vines to the Americas, South Africa, Australia, and New Zealand from the 16th century onward, making wine a global industry.


## Wine in ancient Azerbaijan

Azerbaijan’s evidence is especially strong in Nakhchivan and western Azerbaijan. Excavations along the Arpachay River in Sharur have found fragments of presses and fermentation vats suggesting wine production as much as 6,000 years ago.[^1_1][^1_3]

Other important finds include:

- Jars with wine residue in Göygöl, dating to the second millennium BCE.[^1_10][^1_3]
- Stone fermentation and storage vessels with residue and grape seeds at Kultepe, Karabakhlar, and Galajig, also from the second millennium BCE.[^1_10]
- A wine cellar in Qabala dating to the 1st–3rd centuries CE.[^1_3]
- A reference by the Greek geographer Strabo in the 1st century BCE to wine from the region.[^1_10]

In short, Azerbaijan was not merely a stop on wine’s route outward; it was part of the original Caucasus winemaking world.

## Medieval and early modern Azerbaijan

After the Arab conquest of Azerbaijan in the 7th century and the spread of Islam, open commercial winemaking declined in many areas. Yet thick, honey-like wines continued to be produced, particularly for medicinal purposes and among ruling elites during the Middle Ages.[^1_3]

This period shows an important distinction: religious rules affected public and commercial consumption, but did not erase the region’s deep viticultural knowledge or grape-growing traditions.[^1_3][^1_10]

## The German turning point

Modern Azerbaijani commercial wine began in the 19th century, when German settlers from Württemberg arrived in the South Caucasus under Russian imperial rule around 1817–1819. They founded Helenendorf, now Göygöl, planted vineyards, and brought European winemaking methods.[^1_11][^1_12]

In 1860, Christopher Vohrer established Azerbaijan’s first major commercial winery in Göygöl—often called the Göygöl Wine Plant. This marked the shift from largely household and local production to an organized wine industry.[^1_11][^1_1][^1_3]

## Soviet era and decline

Under Soviet rule, Azerbaijan became a significant wine-producing republic, with large-scale vineyards and production geared partly toward sweet wines favored in Soviet markets.[^1_13][^1_1]

The industry suffered severely in the 1980s during Mikhail Gorbachev’s anti-alcohol campaign, when many vineyards were destroyed or uprooted. German communities had already been expelled during World War II, so the sector lost both historic expertise and much of its planted area.[^1_1]

## Azerbaijan’s modern revival

From the early 2000s, Azerbaijan rebuilt its wine sector through vineyard replanting, foreign expertise, modern equipment, and investment in both international and indigenous grapes.[^1_1]

Key modern varieties include:

- **Madrasa** — a notable indigenous red grape.
- **Bayan Shira** — an indigenous white grape.
- International varieties such as Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Chardonnay, and others, often used alongside local grapes.

The modern emphasis has moved away from the old Soviet-style sweet wines toward drier, more internationally oriented wines, while also using Azerbaijan’s ancient native varieties as a point of distinction.[^1_1]

## Azerbaijan’s place in wine history

| Period | What happened in Azerbaijan |
| :-- | :-- |
| Neolithic / around 6,000 years ago | Presses and fermentation vats near the Arpachay River in Nakhchivan suggest early winemaking. [^1_1][^1_3] |
| Second millennium BCE | Wine residue found in Göygöl jars; fermentation and storage evidence at Kultepe, Karabakhlar, and Galajig. [^1_3][^1_10] |
| 1st century BCE | Strabo refers to local wine. [^1_10] |
| 1st–3rd centuries CE | A wine cellar is recorded in Qabala. [^1_3] |
| 7th century onward | Islamic rule limits public wine consumption, though elite and medicinal use continues. [^1_3] |
| 1817–1860 | German settlers introduce modern commercial viticulture; Vohrer founds Azerbaijan’s first major winery in 1860. [^1_11][^1_3] |
| Soviet period | Large-scale production expands, then collapses during the 1980s anti-alcohol campaign. [^1_1] |
| 2000s–today | Vineyards are replanted and Azerbaijani wine revives around indigenous and international grapes. [^1_1] |

Azerbaijan’s wine history is therefore not a recent story with ancient decoration attached. It belongs to one of the world’s oldest wine regions, and its modern industry is reconnecting with a tradition that likely began thousands of years before the first commercial winery opened in Göygöl.[^1_2][^1_3][^1_1]

# What are native grape varieties in Azerbaijan

Azerbaijan has dozens of native grape varieties, historically around 400 local types, but the two flagship indigenous wine grapes are **Madrasa** for red wine and **Bayan Shira** for white wine. [^2_1][^2_2][^2_3]

## Main native wine grapes

| Variety | Color | Character and use |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Madrasa** (Mədrəsə / Matrassa) | Red | Azerbaijan’s best-known indigenous red grape; thick-skinned and mainly grown around Madrasa village near Shamakhi and in the Ganjabasar region. It makes structured, full-bodied red wines and is also used in blends. [^2_3][^2_2] |
| **Bayan Shira** (Bayanshira / Bayan Shirey) | White | A late-ripening native white grape named after Bayan village in Dashkasan. It produces crisp, relatively neutral whites and has traditionally been important for Azerbaijani sparkling wine. [^2_4][^2_3][^2_5] |
| **Khindogni** (Hindogna) | White | A local technical grape used in winemaking; it is among the more significant native varieties in Azerbaijani vineyards. [^2_6][^2_7] |
| **Gamashara** (Hamashara) | White | A native technical grape, historically important and still present in Azerbaijani vineyard plantings. [^2_6][^2_7] |
| **Shirvanshahi** | White | An indigenous technical variety associated with the historic Shirvan region. [^2_7][^2_6] |
| **Ag Shani** (White Shani) | White | A traditional local variety; along with Gara Shani, it is part of Azerbaijan’s historic grape heritage. [^2_6][^2_8] |
| **Gara Shani** (Black Shani) | Red/black | A native variety traditionally grown in Azerbaijan, though less prominent in modern export wines than Madrasa. [^2_6][^2_8] |
| **Tabrizi** (Tebrizi) | White | An ancient local variety cultivated in several parts of Azerbaijan, including Nakhchivan. [^2_6][^2_9] |
| **Arna-Gırna** (Arna Gna) | Varies | A traditional variety associated with ancient viticulture centers such as Meghri and Zangilan. [^2_6][^2_8] |
| **Marandi** | White | A historic Azerbaijani variety, including the Shamakhi Marandi type. [^2_1][^2_6] |

## Other native and heritage varieties

Azerbaijan’s national viticulture records also list heritage varieties such as **Derbendi, Zeynabi, Misgali, Ganja Pink (Ganja Pembesi), Bendi, Aghdam Kechiemdzhei, Chahrai, Huseyni, Shirvanshahi, Agh Kishmish, Girmizi Kishmish, and Novras**. [^2_8][^2_1]

Some of these are primarily **table grapes** or raisin grapes rather than wine grapes. The country’s viticulture authority notes that Azerbaijan once cultivated roughly 400 local varieties, although only a smaller group now dominates commercial wine production. [^2_1][^2_6]

## Which ones matter most today

For a visitor or wine buyer, the names to remember are:

- **Madrasa** — the signature Azerbaijani red.
- **Bayan Shira** — the signature Azerbaijani white.
- **Khindogni, Gamashara, and Shirvanshahi** — important native technical varieties with strong historical roots. [^2_2][^2_6][^2_7]

Azerbaijani producers increasingly use Madrasa and Bayan Shira as single-varietal wines or blend them with international grapes such as Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, and Chardonnay to express local character. [^2_3]

# How did soviet era affect azerbaijani wine

The Soviet era transformed Azerbaijani wine in two opposite ways: it first industrialized and massively expanded production, then nearly destroyed the sector through Gorbachev’s anti-alcohol campaign. Its legacy is still visible in the scale of vineyards, the loss of old varieties, and the country’s later shift toward quality-focused wine.[^3_1][^3_2]

## Rapid industrial expansion

After Azerbaijan joined the USSR, viticulture and winemaking were nationalized. The state created large collective and state farms, built major processing plants, and organized production for the Soviet-wide market rather than for local quality or terroir.[^3_1]

Azerbaijan became one of the USSR’s major wine-producing republics. Production emphasized high-volume, often sweet table wines and fortified styles; brands such as the port-style **Aghdam** wine became well known across the Soviet Union.[^3_3][^3_1]

By 1984, the industry reached its Soviet peak: more than 2 million tonnes of grapes were harvested from roughly 275,000 hectares of vines.[^3_3][^3_1]

## Quantity over quality

Soviet planning prioritized output, standardized products, and supply to other republics rather than fine-wine production. This meant that many wines were made in large volumes, often sweet or fortified, with less attention to vineyard site, low yields, or grape identity.[^3_4][^3_1]

The system also weakened the connection to Azerbaijan’s indigenous varieties. Large-scale state farms favored grapes and styles that fit Soviet demand, while traditional local plantings were less valued.[^3_1]

## Gorbachev’s anti-alcohol campaign

In 1985, Mikhail Gorbachev launched the USSR-wide anti-alcohol campaign, often called the “dry law.” In Azerbaijan it led to the uprooting of vineyards, closure of wineries, and conversion of some vineyard land to table grapes.[^3_5][^3_1]

The damage was severe:

- Grape harvests fell from about 2 million tonnes in 1984 to about 1 million tonnes in 1991.[^3_2]
- Most vineyards were destroyed, including many indigenous grape plantings.[^3_1]
- Grape-growing land fell from 218,000 hectares in 1985 to under 16,000 hectares by 2014.[^3_5]
- The industry later bottomed out at about 50,000 tonnes of grapes in 2004.[^3_2]


## Post-Soviet collapse and recovery

The USSR’s breakup compounded the damage: state farms and processing systems collapsed, and Azerbaijan’s early post-Soviet economy was in crisis.[^3_6]

Recovery began in earnest from 2002, when Azerbaijan adopted a law on viticulture and winemaking and began replanting vineyards, improving quality, and bringing in expertise from countries such as Italy and Moldova.[^3_7][^3_6]

## Lasting effects

| Effect | Consequence |
| :-- | :-- |
| Industrialization | Azerbaijan gained large-scale vineyards, wineries, and export experience within the USSR. [^3_1] |
| Soviet market orientation | Production favored sweet, fortified, and high-volume wines rather than terroir-driven quality wine. [^3_1][^3_3] |
| Anti-alcohol campaign | Most vineyards were uprooted and many indigenous varieties were lost or severely reduced. [^3_1][^3_5] |
| Post-Soviet disruption | State infrastructure collapsed, leaving a much smaller industry. [^3_2][^3_6] |
| Modern revival | Since 2002, Azerbaijan has rebuilt vineyards and increasingly promoted native grapes such as Madrasa and Bayan Shira alongside international varieties. [^3_7][^3_8] |

In short, the USSR gave Azerbaijan a large but standardized wine industry; the anti-alcohol campaign then dismantled much of it. Today’s Azerbaijani wine revival is partly an effort to recover from that loss and rebuild around both heritage grapes and modern quality standards.[^3_8][^3_7][^3_1]

# Wine, Winemaking, and Azerbaijan: A Source-Based Research Report

This report treats wine’s earliest history as a **Caucasus–Near Eastern development zone**, not a single-point invention, and treats Azerbaijan as a well-attested part of that zone. The strongest established claims are: early wine residues in the South Caucasus and northwestern Iran from the 6th millennium BCE; a purpose-built winery at Areni-1 around 4100 BCE; substantial evidence for ancient viticulture in present-day Azerbaijan; a major Soviet-era expansion and collapse; and a post-2002 revival. Claims that require more caution include the precise meaning of the “6,000-year-old” Nakhchivan evidence, Strabo’s supposed direct reference to Azerbaijani wine, and the extent to which medieval Islamic rule suppressed winemaking.[^4_1][^4_2][^4_3]

## 1. Earliest global evidence for grape wine

The earliest accepted chemical evidence for grape wine comes from pottery residues, not from a complete winery. The three most important early sites are in Georgia, Iran, and Armenia; together they support a broad early winemaking zone rather than one “birthplace.”[^4_4][^4_5][^4_1]


| Site | Location | Approximate date | Evidence | Scholarly interpretation |
| :-- | :-- | --: | :-- | :-- |
| **Shulaveri–Gadachrili** | Kvemo Kartli, Georgia | c. 6000–5800 BCE | Tartaric acid and related wine markers in Neolithic pottery; large buried jars; grape remains | Strong evidence for fermented grape beverage and early viticulture in the South Caucasus. [^4_1][^4_4] |
| **Hajji Firuz Tepe** | Northern Zagros, Iran | c. 5400–5000 BCE | Wine residue in six jars from a Neolithic kitchen/storage area | Early evidence for grape wine outside the Caucasus proper, indicating a wider Near Eastern development zone. [^4_4][^4_5] |
| **Areni-1** | Vayots Dzor, Armenia | c. 4100 BCE | Press/treading platform, vat, fermentation and storage vessels, grape seeds, chemical residues | Oldest known **purpose-built winery**, showing organized production rather than merely incidental fermentation. [^4_2][^4_6] |

The key distinction is this: **Shulaveri–Gadachrili and Hajji Firuz Tepe provide the earliest evidence that wine existed; Areni-1 provides the earliest evidence of a dedicated winemaking facility.** The Georgian evidence is generally treated as the earliest chemical proof of wine, while Areni-1 is the earliest known winery.[^4_2][^4_5][^4_1]

## 2. One origin or a wider development?

The evidence does **not** support a simple claim that wine was invented in one country. It supports a model of gradual development across the South Caucasus, eastern Anatolia, and northwestern Iran, where wild *Vitis vinifera* was available and early farming communities could experiment with fermentation and storage.[^4_7][^4_8][^4_1]

**Established evidence:**

- Chemical wine residues exist in Georgia by c. 6000–5800 BCE.[^4_1]
- Chemical wine residues exist in northwestern Iran by c. 5400–5000 BCE.[^4_5][^4_4]
- A dedicated winemaking installation existed in Armenia by c. 4100 BCE.[^4_2]
- Wild grape populations and early viticulture were present across the Caucasus and adjacent regions.[^4_8][^4_7]

**Reasonable hypotheses, not settled facts:**

- That domesticated grapevine was first domesticated in a single locality.
- That wine spread outward from one “original” site.
- That every early residue necessarily represents the same production method or social use.

The most defensible formulation is: **wine emerged during the Neolithic in the South Caucasus–northern Near East region, with Georgia, Iran, Armenia, eastern Anatolia, and Azerbaijan all part of the early evidence landscape.**[^4_5][^4_8][^4_1]

## 3. Earliest evidence in present-day Azerbaijan

Azerbaijan has significant archaeological evidence for ancient viticulture, but the dating and interpretation of individual finds vary by source. The most frequently cited evidence comes from Nakhchivan’s Arpachay valley, Göygöl, Kültəpə, Qarabulaq, Gələcik, and Qabala.[^4_3][^4_9]


| Site / area | Evidence | Estimated date | Interpretation | Source status |
| :-- | :-- | --: | :-- | :-- |
| **Arpachay River valley, Sharur, Nakhchivan** | Reported fragments of presses and fermentation vats | Often described as c. 6,000 years ago | Potentially wine production; see Section 4 for limits | Widely repeated in Azerbaijani tourism and archaeological summaries; original excavation reports need direct consultation. [^4_10][^4_3] |
| **Göygöl / Khanlar area** | Ceramic jars with wine residue | Second millennium BCE | Strong evidence for stored wine | Repeated in national and academic summaries. [^4_3][^4_9] |
| **Kültəpə** | Stone fermentation and storage vessels, residue, grape seeds | Second millennium BCE | Evidence consistent with winemaking or grape processing | Cited by AZERTAC and Azerbaijani wine-history materials. [^4_9] |
| **Qarabulaq** | Stone vessels associated with wine production | Second millennium BCE | Likely wine-related processing/storage | Cited in Azerbaijani archaeological summaries; details require original reports. [^4_9] |
| **Gələcik** | Stone vessels and grape seeds | Second millennium BCE | Evidence for grape processing or wine production | Cited in Azerbaijani summaries. [^4_9] |
| **Qabala** | Wine cellar | 1st–3rd centuries CE | Organized storage/consumption in antiquity | Cited by Azerbaijan Tourism Board. [^4_3] |
| **Nakhchivan more broadly** | Grape remains, storage jars, presses, and later medieval evidence | Neolithic through medieval periods | Long-term viticultural tradition | Supported by regional archaeology, but individual claims vary in evidentiary strength. [^4_3][^4_9] |

## 4. How strong is the “6,000-year-old Nakhchivan wine” claim?

The claim that wine was made along the Arpachay River in Nakhchivan about 6,000 years ago is **plausible and widely reported, but not yet as firmly established in international scholarship as the Georgian, Iranian, and Armenian early evidence**. The claim appears in Azerbaijani tourism, national wine-history, and journalistic sources, which cite archaeological finds of presses and fermentation vats in Sharur.[^4_10][^4_11][^4_3]

Important qualifications:

- The claim is usually based on **equipment and site interpretation**, not on a widely cited sequence of chemical residue analyses comparable to Georgia’s Shulaveri–Gadachrili results.[^4_10][^4_1]
- Presses, vats, and storage jars can indicate wine production, but they can also relate to grape processing, other fruit liquids, water, dairy, or general storage unless residue analysis identifies wine markers such as tartaric acid and its salts.[^4_1][^4_5]
- “About 6,000 years ago” is a rounded archaeological date; exact site-by-site chronology and dating methods should be confirmed from the original excavation reports.
- The claim is therefore best stated as: **“Archaeological finds in the Arpachay valley are interpreted by Azerbaijani researchers as evidence of wine production around 6,000 years ago; this is credible but requires direct publication-level verification before it can be ranked with Areni-1 or Shulaveri–Gadachrili.”**[^4_11][^4_3][^4_10]


## 5. How ancient wine is identified

Archaeologists rarely rely on one type of evidence alone. They combine chemistry, botanical remains, vessels, and context.

- **Chemical residue analysis:** Looks for tartaric acid and its salts, which occur naturally in grapes; malvidin and other anthocyanins can indicate red wine. This is the strongest method for identifying wine in pottery, but contamination, preservation, and similar organic acids require careful controls.[^4_5][^4_1]
- **Grape seeds and skins:** Establish that grapes were present and can help identify wild versus cultivated forms, but they do not by themselves prove wine.
- **Presses, treading platforms, vats, and storage jars:** Show processing capacity and organization, but equipment alone does not prove the liquid was wine.
- **Context:** A combination of grape remains, wine-specific chemistry, sealed storage, and specialized installations provides the strongest case. Areni-1 is strong because it combines a press, vat, jars, grape remains, and chemical evidence.[^4_6][^4_2]


## 6. Trade and cultural networks

Ancient Azerbaijan and the South Caucasus were connected to Mesopotamia, Iran, Anatolia, Georgia, and the Black Sea through river valleys, mountain passes, the Caspian littoral, and later caravan routes. Wine, grapes, pottery, metals, textiles, and luxury goods moved along these networks.[^4_3][^4_8]

**Established or well-supported connections:**

- The Araxes/Kura corridor linked the South Caucasus with northwestern Iran and Mesopotamia.[^4_8]
- Shared ceramic traditions, storage-jar forms, and viticultural practices across the Caucasus and adjacent Iran point to long-term exchange.[^4_1][^4_5]
- Classical sources describe the Caucasus and Caspian region as agriculturally productive and connected to Armenia, Iberia, Albania, and the Caspian world.[^4_12]
- Later medieval trade integrated Ganja, Barda, Shamakhi, Nakhchivan, and Tabriz into wider Persian, Arab, and Silk Road commercial systems.

**Less secure claims:**

- That a specific grape variety spread along a named prehistoric route.
- That wine exports from ancient Albania can be quantified.
- That modern Azerbaijani grape varieties directly descend from specific ancient vines without genetic and ampelographic evidence.


## 7. Strabo and “Azerbaijani wine”

Modern popular sources often say that Strabo mentioned wine from present-day Azerbaijan in the 1st century BCE. The underlying passage is real, but it should be cited carefully.

Strabo’s *Geography* **11.14.4** describes Armenia and neighboring regions. In Horace Leonard Jones’s translation, Strabo says that the Araxes flows to the extremities of Albania and empties into the Caspian; he then describes Sacasene bordering Albania and the Cyrus River, and says that the district “abounds with products of the soil, cultivated fruit trees and evergreens.”[^4_12]

**What this supports:** Strabo attests to fertile, cultivated land in the Albania–Armenia–Caspian borderlands, including fruit cultivation.[^4_12]

**What it does not directly prove:** The cited passage does not explicitly say, “The Albanians made wine” or “Azerbaijani wine was exported.” Therefore, claims that Strabo directly documented Azerbaijani wine should be qualified. The safest formulation is: **Strabo describes the fertile, cultivated landscape of the region associated with ancient Caucasian Albania and neighboring Armenia; he does not, in the commonly cited passage, provide an explicit statement about Albanian wine production.**[^4_12]

## 8. Islam, the Arab conquest, and wine

The Arab conquest of Azerbaijan in the 7th–8th centuries brought Islamic legal norms that discouraged or prohibited wine drinking for Muslims. However, religious prohibition did not necessarily eliminate viticulture, grape production, medicinal wine, non-Muslim consumption, or elite use.[^4_9][^4_3]

A careful distinction is essential:

- **Viticulture** — growing grapes — could continue for table grapes, raisins, grape syrup, vinegar, medicine, and other uses.
- **Wine production** — could persist in limited, local, medicinal, elite, or non-Muslim contexts.
- **Public commercial wine consumption** — was more likely to decline where Islamic norms were enforced.

Azerbaijani wine-history sources state that after the Arab conquest, thick, honey-like wines continued to be produced, particularly for medicinal purposes and among elites. This is plausible but should be treated as a general historical interpretation unless supported by specific medieval textual citations.[^4_3]

## 9. Medieval written sources

Medieval geographers and historians do describe the towns and agricultural wealth of the South Caucasus, including Ganja, Barda, Shamakhi, and Nakhchivan. However, many popular claims about “medieval Azerbaijani wine” rely on generalized references to grapes, gardens, and prosperity rather than explicit winemaking passages.


| Author | Work | Relevance | Caution |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Al-Masudi** | *Muruj al-Dhahab* (“Meadows of Gold and Mines of Gems”) | Describes the Caucasus, Caspian region, and its peoples and trade | Direct wine-production passages for Azerbaijani towns require exact edition/page verification. |
| **Ibn Hawqal** | *Kitab Surat al-Ard* (“Book of the Configuration of the Earth”) | Describes Arran, Azerbaijan, Barda, and regional agriculture and trade | His descriptions establish economic and agricultural context, not necessarily wine production. |
| **Al-Muqaddasi** | *Ahsan al-Taqasim fi Ma‘rifat al-Aqalim* (“The Best Divisions for Knowledge of the Regions”) | Describes towns, products, and routes of the Islamic world | Useful for regional agriculture and commerce; specific wine claims need direct citation. |
| **Abu’l-Fida** | *Taqwim al-Buldan* (“A Sketch of the Countries”) | Later medieval geographical compilation describing Azerbaijan and adjacent regions | Often used as a secondary geographical source, not a direct winemaking source. |

**Assessment:** Medieval sources can support the continuity of viticulture and agricultural prosperity in Azerbaijan, but claims that they explicitly document flourishing wine production in Ganja, Barda, or Shamakhi need exact quotations from critical editions. Until those passages are supplied, they should be treated as contextual rather than direct evidence.

## 10. Historical Azerbaijani wine terms

The terms **mey, sharab, nabiz, and chakhir** reflect the layered Persian, Arabic, and Turkic linguistic history of the region.

- **Mey** — Persian-derived term for wine or intoxicating drink; in classical Persian poetry it often carries literary and Sufi meanings beyond simple alcohol.
- **Sharab** — Arabic/Persian term for wine; in modern Azerbaijani, *şərab* is the standard word for wine.
- **Nabiz** — Arabic term traditionally used for a drink made from soaked dates, raisins, or grapes; its legal status and alcohol content varied by preparation and time.
- **Chakhir** — from a Turkic/Armenian-adjacent wine vocabulary; in modern usage it can mean wine, but historical usage varied by region and community.

The main point is that **modern “wine” terminology cannot be projected backward without checking the source’s language, date, and legal-religious context**. A medieval mention of *nabiz* is not automatically a mention of fermented grape wine.

## 11. German settlement and Helenendorf

German settlers from Württemberg began migrating to the South Caucasus under Russian imperial rule in the late 1810s. The commonly cited figure is about **500 families** arriving from 1817–1819; they founded **Helenendorf**, present-day Göygöl, and **Annenfeld**, present-day Shamkir.[^4_13][^4_14]

Their contribution was decisive for modern Azerbaijani commercial wine:

- They planted European-style vineyards.
- They introduced organized cellar practices and commercial wine production.
- Helenendorf became the center of Azerbaijan’s German wine economy.[^4_14][^4_13]

The claim that Germans “invented” Azerbaijani wine is incorrect; they **modernized and commercialized** an already ancient viticultural tradition.[^4_13][^4_3]

## 12. The first commercial winery in Göygöl

The standard claim is that **Christopher/Christoff Vohrer** established Azerbaijan’s first commercial winery in Helenendorf/Göygöl in **1860**. This date is repeated by Azerbaijan Tourism Board, National Geographic, and industry sources.[^4_10][^4_13][^4_3]

**Assessment:**

- The 1860 date is credible as the conventional founding date of the Vohrer winery.
- “Azerbaijan’s first winery” should be qualified: it was likely the first **large-scale commercial** winery in the modern sense, not the first place wine was ever made in Azerbaijan.
- Earlier household, monastic, local, and small-scale production certainly existed.
- Detailed production and export figures for the early Vohrer enterprise require archival research in Azerbaijani, German, and Russian imperial records; the public sources reviewed here do not provide a reliable early production series.[^4_13][^4_3][^4_10]


## 13. Deportation of Germans in World War II

Soviet authorities deported ethnic Germans from the South Caucasus in 1941, after Germany invaded the USSR. This removed many of the families and specialists who had sustained Helenendorf’s wine economy.[^4_10][^4_13]

The direct effect on Azerbaijani wine was the loss of:

- Experienced vineyard and cellar workers.
- Family-owned winemaking knowledge.
- Local commercial networks.
- Continuity in grape-growing and winery management.

Public sources establish the deportation and its general disruptive effect, but a precise inventory of confiscated vineyards, wineries, and equipment would require Soviet archival records.[^4_13][^4_10]

## 14. Russian Empire and Soviet transformation

Under the Russian Empire, commercial winemaking expanded around German settlements, especially Helenendorf and Annenfeld. Under the USSR, the industry was nationalized and reorganized into large state farms and processing enterprises.[^4_15][^4_13]

Key Soviet-era features:

- **Nationalization:** A State Committee for viticulture and winemaking oversaw the sector; sovkhoz and kolkhoz systems organized production.[^4_15]
- **Expansion:** Azerbaijan became one of the USSR’s leading wine-producing republics, with production peaking in 1984.[^4_16][^4_17]
- **Style:** The system favored quantity, sweet wines, and fortified wines for Soviet markets rather than fine-wine quality.[^4_18][^4_15]
- **Scale:** At the 1984 peak, Azerbaijan reportedly harvested more than 2 million tonnes of grapes from roughly 275,000 hectares.[^4_18][^4_15]


## 15. The 1985 anti-alcohol campaign

Gorbachev’s 1985 anti-alcohol campaign caused a catastrophic contraction of Azerbaijani viticulture. Vineyards were uprooted, wineries closed, and wine grapes were replaced in some areas by table grapes.[^4_19][^4_15]

Reported effects include:

- Grape harvest fell from about **2 million tonnes in 1984** to about **1 million tonnes in 1991**.[^4_17]
- Most vineyards were destroyed during the campaign.[^4_20][^4_15]
- One source reports **2,083 hectares** destroyed in Azerbaijan during the prohibition period, though this figure appears low relative to the broader collapse and should be treated cautiously.[^4_21]
- Grape-growing area later fell from **218,000 hectares in 1985** to under **16,000 hectares by 2014**.[^4_19]

The overall conclusion is secure: the campaign, combined with the Soviet collapse, nearly destroyed Azerbaijan’s commercial wine sector. Exact vineyard-by-vineyard loss figures require archival confirmation.[^4_17][^4_15][^4_19]

## 16. Madrasa and Bayan Shira

**Madrasa** and **Bayan Shira** are Azerbaijan’s two most prominent indigenous wine grapes, but they are not the country’s only native varieties.[^4_22][^4_23][^4_24]

- **Madrasa** is a red wine grape associated with the Shamakhi region and the village of Madrasa; it is described in Azerbaijani ampelographic literature as a local variety of national selection.[^4_25][^4_26]
- **Bayan Shira** is a white grape originating in the Ganja–Dashkasan zone, particularly associated with Bayan village; research describes it as indigenous and late-ripening.[^4_24][^4_27]
- Other local varieties include **Khindogni, Gamashara, Shirvanshahi, Ag Shani, Gara Shani, Tabrizi, Arna-Gırna, Marandi, Derbendi, and Zeynabi**.[^4_23][^4_28][^4_29]

**Assessment of the “two indigenous grapes” claim:** It is too narrow. Madrasa and Bayan Shira are the two flagship indigenous wine varieties, but Azerbaijan has many more local varieties, some of which are table grapes rather than wine grapes.[^4_30][^4_22][^4_23]

## 17. Revival since the 2000s

Azerbaijan began rebuilding its wine sector from **2002**, following a law on viticulture and winemaking. The revival involved replanting vineyards, modernizing wineries, attracting foreign expertise, and reintroducing local varieties.[^4_16][^4_20]

Modern developments include:

- New private wineries and renewed investment in Shamakhi, Ismayilli, Qabala, Göygöl, Shamkir, and Nakhchivan.[^4_31][^4_10]
- Use of Madrasa, Bayan Shira, Khindogni, and other local grapes alongside international varieties.[^4_32][^4_33]
- A shift from Soviet-style sweet wines toward dry still wines, sparkling wines, and export-oriented branding.[^4_18][^4_10]

Official production and export statistics should be distinguished from promotional claims. Public tourism and wine-industry sources are useful for narrative history but are not substitutes for State Statistical Committee data or customs records.

## 18. Exports and international position

Over the past 10–15 years, Azerbaijan has moved from a largely domestic and post-Soviet market toward selective exports to Europe, Asia, and neighboring markets. Its international positioning emphasizes ancient Caucasus heritage, indigenous grapes, and modern winemaking.[^4_32][^4_10]

However, available public sources do not provide a complete, consistently comparable export series. A rigorous export assessment would need:

- Azerbaijani State Statistical Committee production data.
- Customs export data by volume, value, and destination.
- Independent market reports.
- Separate treatment of bottled wine, bulk wine, grape concentrate, and brandy.

Promotional claims about “ancient 8,000-year-old wine” should not be treated as export evidence; they are heritage branding.

## 20. Assessment of the original claims

| Claim | Assessment |
| :-- | :-- |
| **Wine originated in the Caucasus** | Partially supported. The earliest evidence is concentrated in the South Caucasus and nearby northwestern Iran, but “origin” should be plural and regional. [^4_1][^4_5][^4_8] |
| **Azerbaijan has 6,000-year-old winemaking** | Plausible but not yet as internationally consolidated as Georgian, Iranian, or Armenian evidence; equipment-based claims require residue analysis and original reports. [^4_10][^4_3][^4_11] |
| **Strabo mentioned Azerbaijani wine** | Needs qualification. Strabo describes the fertile Albania–Armenia borderlands, but the cited passage does not explicitly mention Albanian wine. [^4_12] |
| **Islam caused winemaking to decline** | Broadly plausible for public/commercial consumption, but it did not end viticulture or all wine production; evidence must distinguish grapes, medicinal wine, elite use, and non-Muslim consumption. [^4_3][^4_9] |
| **Madrasa and Bayan Shira are Azerbaijan’s two indigenous grapes** | Misleading if stated as “only two.” They are the two flagship indigenous wine grapes; Azerbaijan has many additional native varieties. [^4_22][^4_30][^4_23] |
| **Germans created Azerbaijani wine** | Incorrect. Germans created the modern commercial wine industry; wine itself has much older roots in Azerbaijan. [^4_13][^4_3] |
| **Soviets built then destroyed the industry** | Strongly supported. Soviet planning expanded production; the 1985 anti-alcohol campaign devastated vineyards and wineries. [^4_15][^4_17][^4_19] |

## 21. Concise chronology

- **c. 6000–5800 BCE:** Earliest chemical evidence for grape wine at Shulaveri–Gadachrili, Georgia.[^4_1]
- **c. 5400–5000 BCE:** Wine residues at Hajji Firuz Tepe, Iran.[^4_4][^4_5]
- **c. 4100 BCE:** Areni-1 winery, Armenia.[^4_2]
- **c. 6,000 years ago:** Azerbaijani researchers interpret Arpachay/Sharur finds as evidence of early winemaking; strength of claim requires original reports.[^4_3][^4_10]
- **Second millennium BCE:** Wine residue and processing evidence at Göygöl, Kültəpə, Qarabulaq, and Gələcik.[^4_9][^4_3]
- **1st century BCE:** Strabo describes the fertile Albania–Armenia borderlands.[^4_12]
- **1st–3rd centuries CE:** Wine cellar at Qabala.[^4_3]
- **7th–8th centuries:** Arab conquest and Islam reshape public wine consumption; viticulture continues in altered forms.[^4_3]
- **1817–1819:** Württemberg Germans settle in the South Caucasus; Helenendorf and Annenfeld founded.[^4_14][^4_13]
- **1860:** Conventional date for Vohrer’s commercial winery in Helenendorf/Göygöl.[^4_10][^4_3]
- **1941:** Deportation of South Caucasus Germans disrupts the wine economy.[^4_13][^4_10]
- **Soviet era to 1984:** Industrial expansion and peak production.[^4_15][^4_16]
- **1985:** Anti-alcohol campaign devastates vineyards.[^4_19][^4_15]
- **2002 onward:** Legal and institutional basis for revival; vineyard replanting and modernization.[^4_20][^4_16]
- **2010s–2020s:** Renewed focus on Madrasa, Bayan Shira, and export-oriented quality wine.[^4_31][^4_32]
