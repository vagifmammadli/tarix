# the history of wine and how it's spread in the world and especially Azerbaijan. Also about the history of azerbaijani wine since the first wine

Wine likely began in the wider Caucasus–northern Near East region roughly 8,000 years ago, and Azerbaijan sits at the heart of that early winemaking zone. Its own wine story stretches from Neolithic and Bronze Age evidence, through medieval restrictions under Islamic rule, to German-led commercialization in the 19th century and a modern revival since the 2000s.[^1_1][^1_2][^1_3]

## Where wine began

The earliest known traces of grape wine come from the South Caucasus and nearby northwestern Iran. Chemical residues in pottery from Georgia’s Shulaveri-Gadachrili area date to about 6000–5800 BCE, while jars from Hajji Firuz Tepe in Iran date to roughly 5400–5000 BCE.[^1_2][^1_4][^1_5]

The oldest known purpose-built winery is Areni-1 in Armenia, dated to about 4100 BCE; it included a press, fermentation vessels, storage jars, and chemical evidence of red wine. This does not mean wine was “invented” in one village—rather, grape domestication and fermentation likely developed across the Caucasus, eastern Anatolia, the Levant, and northwestern Iran.[^1_6][^1_7][^1_8]

## How wine spread

Wine spread along trade, migration, and imperial networks from its Caucasian and Near Eastern heartland.

- **Mesopotamia and Egypt:** Wine became a prestige drink in elite and religious settings; Egyptian evidence shows its use by rulers and in funerary contexts.[^1_9]
- **Greece and the Aegean:** Greeks integrated wine into daily life, symposia, trade, and religion, spreading vines across the Mediterranean.[^1_9]
- **Roman world:** Rome expanded viticulture through Europe—especially present-day France, Spain, Italy, Germany, and the Balkans—creating many of the foundations of modern European wine regions.[^1_9]
- **Islamic world:** After the spread of Islam, alcohol was restricted in many Muslim societies, but viticulture and wine knowledge persisted in some regions, often for medicinal, elite, minority-community, or local traditional uses.[^1_3]
- **Global expansion:** European colonization carried *Vitis vinifera* vines to the Americas, South Africa, Australia, and New Zealand from the 16th century onward, making wine a global industry.


## Wine in ancient Azerbaijan

Azerbaijan’s evidence is especially strong in Nakhchivan and western Azerbaijan. Excavations along the Arpachay River in Sharur have found fragments of presses and fermentation vats suggesting wine production as much as 6,000 years ago.[^1_1][^1_3]

Other important finds include:

- Jars with wine residue in Göygöl, dating to the second millennium BCE.[^1_10][^1_3]
- Stone fermentation and storage vessels with residue and grape seeds at Kultepe, Karabakhlar, and Galajig, also from the second millennium BCE.[^1_10]
- A wine cellar in Qabala dating to the 1st–3rd centuries CE.[^1_3]
- A reference by the Greek geographer Strabo in the 1st century BCE to wine from the region.[^1_10]

In short, Azerbaijan was not merely a stop on wine’s route outward; it was part of the original Caucasus winemaking world.

## Medieval and early modern Azerbaijan

After the Arab conquest of Azerbaijan in the 7th century and the spread of Islam, open commercial winemaking declined in many areas. Yet thick, honey-like wines continued to be produced, particularly for medicinal purposes and among ruling elites during the Middle Ages.[^1_3]

This period shows an important distinction: religious rules affected public and commercial consumption, but did not erase the region’s deep viticultural knowledge or grape-growing traditions.[^1_3][^1_10]

## The German turning point

Modern Azerbaijani commercial wine began in the 19th century, when German settlers from Württemberg arrived in the South Caucasus under Russian imperial rule around 1817–1819. They founded Helenendorf, now Göygöl, planted vineyards, and brought European winemaking methods.[^1_11][^1_12]

In 1860, Christopher Vohrer established Azerbaijan’s first major commercial winery in Göygöl—often called the Göygöl Wine Plant. This marked the shift from largely household and local production to an organized wine industry.[^1_11][^1_1][^1_3]

## Soviet era and decline

Under Soviet rule, Azerbaijan became a significant wine-producing republic, with large-scale vineyards and production geared partly toward sweet wines favored in Soviet markets.[^1_13][^1_1]

The industry suffered severely in the 1980s during Mikhail Gorbachev’s anti-alcohol campaign, when many vineyards were destroyed or uprooted. German communities had already been expelled during World War II, so the sector lost both historic expertise and much of its planted area.[^1_1]

## Azerbaijan’s modern revival

From the early 2000s, Azerbaijan rebuilt its wine sector through vineyard replanting, foreign expertise, modern equipment, and investment in both international and indigenous grapes.[^1_1]

Key modern varieties include:

- **Madrasa** — a notable indigenous red grape.
- **Bayan Shira** — an indigenous white grape.
- International varieties such as Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Chardonnay, and others, often used alongside local grapes.

The modern emphasis has moved away from the old Soviet-style sweet wines toward drier, more internationally oriented wines, while also using Azerbaijan’s ancient native varieties as a point of distinction.[^1_1]

Azerbaijan’s wine history is therefore not a recent story with ancient decoration attached. It belongs to one of the world’s oldest wine regions, and its modern industry is reconnecting with a tradition that likely began thousands of years before the first commercial winery opened in Göygöl.[^1_2][^1_3][^1_1]
