# Museum Storyline

## The exhibition’s central question

**What can we know about the history of wine, and what do the sources allow us to say about Azerbaijan’s past?**

Visitors are not given a lecture as a fixed answer. They move through a map, compare finds, open sources, and distinguish evidence from interpretation.

## Core idea

The history of wine is not presented as a single invention by one people or village. It is a changing story of grape cultivation, fermentation, storage, trade, and taste across different periods. The South Caucasus is one of the regions with the earliest known evidence for winemaking. Azerbaijan also has an ancient history of viticulture, but individual finds do not by themselves prove uninterrupted production or a single point of origin. The development of commercial winemaking in Göygöl in the 19th century is a new chapter in this story, not its beginning.

## The visitor’s transformation

Visitors arrive asking, “Where did wine begin?” They leave asking a more precise question: “Which find and source supports this claim?” The museum aims not only to share historical information, but to show how to examine it.

## The route

### 0. Threshold — A trace on a vessel

**Visitor question:** What can a stain on an ancient vessel tell us?

**On screen:** Show an authentic find or a sourced 3D model. Do not reveal the whole explanation immediately. Invite visitors to rotate the vessel and select a marked area.

**Interaction:** Compare “grapes,” “fermentation,” and “wine” by asking what evidence would be needed for each interpretation.

**Presenter’s line:** “Can we know what was inside this vessel just by looking at it?”

**Transition:** Visitors move on to learn how evidence is identified.

### 1. Reading the evidence — What does each trace tell us?

**Content:** Explain the difference between chemical residues, grape seeds, presses or fermentation equipment, storage vessels, and written sources. Evidence for grapes does not automatically prove winemaking; evidence for wine consumption does not prove local production.

**Interaction:** Visitors sort examples into “grape cultivation,” “possible evidence of fermentation or wine,” and “direct evidence for wine.” Instead of marking answers simply right or wrong, the museum explains what additional evidence would help.

**Presenter’s line:** “Let’s look at the earliest finds without treating every kind of evidence as equal.”

### 2. The earliest traces — A regional story

**Content:** Present research on Georgia’s Shulaveri–Gadachrili sites, Iran’s Hajji Firuz Tepe, and Armenia’s Areni-1 separately. Distinguish the “earliest chemical traces” from the “oldest known purpose-built winery.”

**Interaction:** Place the sites on a timeline and compare whether each is supported by chemical analysis, equipment, or a built production area.

**Interpretive note:** These finds provide evidence about early wine and winemaking, but they do not justify naming one place as the single origin of wine worldwide.

**Presenter’s line:** “What evidence do we have within this wider region, in the territory of present-day Azerbaijan?”

### 3. Azerbaijan on the map — The story at each site

**Content:** Map Nakhchivan, Göygöl, and other sites only when supported by archaeological sources. Each point shows the find, period, source, and type of evidence.

**Interaction:** Visitors select a location and open three panels: “what was found,” “what it supports,” and “what it cannot prove by itself.”

**The 6,000-year Nakhchivan claim:** Do not present this as settled fact unless the primary archaeological report and evidence have been verified. Until then, either mark it clearly as an interpretation reported by local sources that is still being checked, or leave it off the main timeline.

**Presenter’s line:** “The history of this region is not limited to today’s borders.”

### 4. A region before modern borders — Connections and sources

**Content:** Discuss trade, neighboring societies, routes, and ancient descriptions without equating the Caucasus of the past with modern national borders. If Strabo is included, show the actual passage in *Geography* 11.14.4 and clarify that it does not directly mention wine.

**Interaction:** Visitors compare “what the source says” with “how later writers interpreted it.”

**Presenter’s line:** “Did agriculture and wine culture remain unchanged for centuries?”

### 5. The medieval period — Rules, use, and continuity

**Content:** Examine religious rules, public consumption, elite use, and medicinal references in their specific historical contexts. Keep grape growing, wine production, and drinking separate.

**Interaction:** Match three claims—“grapes were cultivated,” “wine was produced,” and “wine was consumed”—to the kinds of sources that could support them.

**Interpretive note:** Avoid broad claims such as “wine disappeared after Islam.” State the place and period covered by the evidence and make uncertainty visible.

**Presenter’s line:** “A new period brought new production methods and markets.”

### 6. Göygöl/Helenendorf — A new chapter in commercial production

**Content:** Tell the story of 19th-century German settlement, Helenendorf (now Göygöl), the Vohrer winery, and commercial production. Explain this as a new phase of organized industry, not the origin of local winemaking.

**Interaction:** Compare a historical map with the present-day location. If a suitable source is found, use a 3D or photographic exhibit of a cellar or winery.

**Presenter’s line:** “How large did this industry become in the next century?”

### 7. The Soviet period — Expansion and dependence

**Content:** Show changes in state planning, vineyard area, and production only where reliable statistics are available. Explain the limits of sources describing which wine styles were made for the Soviet market.

**Interaction:** Select a year to view production or vineyard figures. Keep the source, unit, and coverage visible beside the chart.

**Presenter’s line:** “Was that growth stable?”

### 8. After 1985 — Disruption and consequences

**Content:** Describe the Soviet anti-alcohol campaign and its effect on vineyards and the industry. Use figures only when supported by evidence; do not fill gaps with estimates presented as fact.

**Interaction:** If reliable before-and-after data exist, let visitors compare them on a timeline. If they do not, show the source gaps and conflicting figures as an open question.

**Presenter’s line:** “What remained after the decline, and what had to be rebuilt?”

### 9. Revival and local grapes — Using heritage today

**Content:** Introduce developments since the 2000s, investment, and grape varieties using current sources and statistics. Present Madrasa and Bayan Shira as familiar examples without implying that they are the only local varieties.

**Interaction:** Each grape card separates growing region, wine use, documented history, and present-day use. Decide whether to add a 3D view after a suitable model has been found.

**Presenter’s line:** “An attractive story about heritage still needs evidence we can check.”

### 10. Closing — Follow the evidence, not a single origin point

**Content:** Return to the opening question, “Where did wine begin?” The final screen does not assign a single origin to one nation. It concludes that the South Caucasus is a region with important early evidence and that Azerbaijan has a layered history of archaeology, viticulture, and commercial production.

**Interaction:** Ask “What can you now say with confidence?” Offer three statements:

- “All wine began in one place here.”
- “This region has important early evidence for wine.”
- “We need to check what the evidence actually shows.”

Explain the difference and provide the source library and further reading.

## Linking the live presentation to the museum

Every museum section has its own link and QR code. The presenter shows the relevant QR code when introducing a section. Visitors read at their own pace; there is no forced task or automatic movement that competes with the presenter.

Suggested 15-minute presentation route:

1. Opening and “what counts as evidence?” — 2 minutes.
2. Early evidence worldwide — 3 minutes.
3. Azerbaijan archaeological map — 3 minutes.
4. Medieval period and commercial industry — 3 minutes.
5. Soviet period, disruption, and revival — 2 minutes.
6. Open question and return to sources — 2 minutes.

Visitors do not have to read every panel. Provide a one-tap “return to the current section” control.

## Decisions before implementation

- Reading level and duration for school-age audiences.
- Whether a primary report for the early Arpachay finds can be located.
- Verified locations and sources for sites on the map.
- Availability of comparable production and vineyard statistics.
- First 3D exhibit: an archaeological scan, a historical space, or a clearly labeled reconstruction.
- Whether QR links will be printed or shown on presentation slides.
